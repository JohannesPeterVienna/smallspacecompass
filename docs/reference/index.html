How to read the table

The table mixes documents of very different weight, on purpose: there is no single rulebook for furnishing a small home, and the figures bracket each other. A 28 in hallway is legal in a U.S. manufactured home; 36 in is the walking-surface width of the ADA Standards; England’s guidance for adaptable homes asks for 900 mm (35.4 in). None of them governs where you put your sofa. Read each entry with its scope:

How it was compiled

Each figure was read in the document itself on 2026-11-18, in the edition named above, and entered with its section or guideline number. Where a document gives both units (the ADA Standards and the NKBA guidelines do), both are copied as printed – which is why 36 in appears as 915 mm in one row and 914 mm in another. Where it gives one, the other column is our conversion and is shaded.

Three limits apply. First, editions: codes are revised, and the edition in force where you live may differ. Second, the International Residential Code: its figures appear here only as quoted in the NKBA guidelines, which state that their code references are based on the 2015 edition; we could not open the code text itself and therefore list no IRC figure on our own account. Third, mattress and furniture sizes: no standard we could read fixes them, so the only furniture sizes in the table are the ones England’s Approved Document M prescribes for checking a layout drawing.

Using a figure

To test one of these widths against your own room, the Clearance Check does the subtraction and compares the result with the matching rows; the Clearance Planner measures whole routes. If you find an entry that does not match its source, write to office@adstern.com with the entry and the section – corrections are made in the table and noted on the About page.
