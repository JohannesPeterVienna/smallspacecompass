12 × 10 ft bedroom with a queen bed on the long wall

In a 12 × 10 ft room, a queen bed fits best with its headboard on the 10 ft wall, so both long sides keep a 24 in strip and the dresser has room for its drawers.

Why it is laid out this way

The room is 144 × 120 in (3.66 × 3.05 m), 120 sq ft. With the headboard against the left (10 ft) wall, the 80 in bed length runs across the 12 ft dimension and leaves 64 in at the foot – space for the door and for the dresser's 18 in drawer zone. Both long sides of the bed keep a 24 in walk-through strip, and the nightstands stand at the head of those strips.

The bedroom door is in the bottom wall near the right-hand corner and swings in towards the corner, away from the bed. The dresser stands against the top wall at the foot end of the room, with its drawer zone pointing down into the room.

Turning the bed so that its headboard sits on the 12 ft wall would leave only 40 in at its foot, enough to walk past but not for a dresser with open drawers. The 12 × 10 bedroom guide compares both orientations and the full-size alternative.

Variants

  • One nightstand. Leaving out the lower nightstand frees the corner beside the door for a narrow chair or a laundry basket.
  • Tall chest instead of dresser. A 30 in wide chest of drawers takes less wall but has the same 18 in drawer zone.
  • Full bed. A full bed (53 × 75 in) in the same orientation gives 5 in more at the foot and 7 in more across the room – see the full bed and desk layout for a different use of that space.

Common mistakes

  • Headboard on the 12 ft wall. Only 40 in remain at the foot: walkable, but not with a dresser and open drawers.
  • A king-size bed. At 76 in wide it leaves only 44 in for both sides in the 10 ft dimension – less than two 24 in strips, before any nightstand.
  • A door that swings towards the bed. If your door is hinged the other way, its arc reaches the foot of the bed; rehanging it may be possible, but ask your landlord first.

With 12 in (30 cm) less

We also ran it with 12 in less in one direction and then the other, the furniture on the shortened side moving with its wall. This plan has no slack. At 11 × 10 ft the foot of the bed keeps 52 in, but the dresser's drawer zone and the bedside strip run into each other. At 12 × 9 ft the 108 in dimension is exactly a 60 in queen plus two 24 in strips, so the nightstands no longer fit. In either smaller room, use a full bed or give up the dresser.

Adapting it to your room

To adapt the plan, check your door first: its position and swing decide whether the dresser fits at the foot of the bed. Then enter your own bed frame – frames are often several inches larger than the mattress – and watch the 24 in strips on both long sides. If they drop below 24 in, choose narrower nightstands or a frame without a wide headboard before you give up the second side. The 12 × 10 bedroom guide compares the queen and full options in more detail, and the Storage Wall Audit helps if the dresser has to go and storage moves up the wall.

Measure the frame, not the mattress label: a padded headboard can add several inches to the length.

Where the allowances come from

A queen mattress is 60 × 80 in. Two 24 in bedside strips – this site's allowance for making the bed – plus the bed take 108 in of the 120 in wall, leaving 12 in of spare. The 18 in drawer zone is the space for an open drawer plus a person standing in front of it, also a planning allowance; 36 in is the ADA reference for routes. The bedroom door is drawn 30 in wide with its full quarter-circle swing; a 32 in door takes 2 in more of the corner beside the door, where the lower nightstand already sits close to the arc. The window on the right-hand wall needs no clearance of its own in this plan, because its route runs through open floor.
