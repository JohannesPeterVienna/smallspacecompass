18 × 14 ft studio, full bed in a sleeping alcove

A bookshelf placed at right angles to the wall turns the top-right corner into a sleeping alcove. The full bed fits behind it with a 24 in walk-in strip, while the loveseat, table and desk share the rest of the room.

Why it is laid out this way

At 216 × 168 in (5.49 × 4.27 m) this studio is 252 sq ft. The door sits on the bottom wall, roughly in the middle, and swings in towards the right. The bed is pushed into the top-right corner so that only its left side is open; the 62 in bookshelf, 12 in deep, stands at right angles to the top wall and screens the bed from the living area without blocking daylight from the left-hand window.

A full bed (53 × 75 in) rather than a queen leaves 47 in between the bed's open side and the shelf, which is enough for a 24 in bedside strip plus a comfortable approach. With a queen (60 × 80 in) that space would drop to 40 in and the alcove would start to feel like a corridor – see Full vs. queen in a small bedroom.

The living side is arranged around the window: loveseat against the top wall, coffee table in front, and a two-person table just below the window with its chair zone pointing into the room. The small desk sits against the right wall below the alcove, with its chair backing into open floor rather than into the route from the door.

Because the divider is a freestanding shelf, anchor it to the wall or ceiling – a tall, narrow bookcase standing on its own can tip.

Variants

  • Curtain instead of shelf. A ceiling-track curtain takes almost no floor. It gives back the 12 in of the shelf's depth to the route but none of its storage.
  • Shorter divider. A shelf of 48 in instead of 62 in opens the alcove towards the window. The bed is then visible from the sofa, but the approach to the bed gets wider.
  • Desk in the alcove. A narrow 18 in deep desk at the foot of the bed fits only if its chair zone does not reach into the 24 in bedside strip; in this room it does, which is why the desk sits on the right wall below the alcove.

Common mistakes

  • A queen in the alcove. The space between the bed's open side and the shelf falls from 47 in to 40 in – still walkable, but the bedside strip and the approach overlap.
  • An unanchored divider. A 62 in bookcase standing at right angles to the wall has no wall behind it. Fix it to the wall or ceiling.
  • A sofa deeper than 34 in. The loveseat and the 18 in legroom in front of it set where the coffee table goes, and the coffee table sets the route to the window.

With 12 in (30 cm) less

We recomputed the plan with the room 12 in smaller and the far-side furniture moved with the wall. At 17 × 14 ft the result hardly changes: the route to the bed is about 63 in, the desk about 47 in and the window 40 in. At 18 × 13 ft the window route drops to about 28 in – below the 32 in short-pinch allowance of the ADA reference, and too tight to pass the coffee table comfortably – and the route to the bed narrows to about 57 in. In a 13 ft deep version, choose a smaller coffee table or move the two-person table to the right wall.

Adapting it to your room

To adapt the plan, start with the divider. Enter your own shelf length and depth and check the gap to the open side of the bed: it should hold the 24 in strip plus room to turn in. Then move the coffee table until the window route is back above 36 in. If your entry door is not in the middle of the bottom wall, check its arc against the two-person table first; that table is the piece closest to the door. The studio layout guide compares the alcove with other ways of separating the bed.

A practical test before buying a divider: mark its footprint on the floor with painter's tape and walk from the door to the bed and to the window a few times.

Why these measurements

A full bed is 53 × 75 in, a queen 60 × 80 in – the mattress sizes the furniture guides use. The 24 in bedside strip and the 32 in chair zones are this site's planning allowances; the 12 in shelf depth is typical for a bookcase. The 36 in reference is the ADA clear width for an accessible route, used as a comfortable target in a home.
