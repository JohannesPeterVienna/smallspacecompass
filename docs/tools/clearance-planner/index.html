How the planner measures a walkway

Most free room planners answer one question: does the furniture fit on the floor? That is the easy part. The hard part is the space you need to use the furniture and to get past it – the arc of the door as it swings open, the 30-odd inches a desk chair rolls back, the strip beside a bed you need for making it, the drawer that has to come out of a dresser. The Clearance Planner draws all of those as zones, then measures the space that is left.

Behind the drawing, the floor is cut into a fine grid of about one-inch cells. Every cell covered by furniture, by a use zone or by the swing of the door is marked as blocked. For every free cell the planner then computes the exact straight-line distance to the nearest blocked cell or wall (a Euclidean distance transform). That distance is the radius of the largest circle you could stand in at that point; twice the radius is the width of the passage there.

Next it searches for a route from the entry door to each target – every piece you marked “measure the walkway to this piece”, plus every window. It does not take the shortest route but the widest one: the route whose narrowest point is as wide as possible (a so-called bottleneck path). Among all routes of that width it then takes the shortest, using an A* search, and draws it in yellow. The point where the route is tightest gets a marker, and the planner measures the gap there once more, exactly, between the two surfaces that cause it. That figure is what you see in the result box.

The target itself and its own use zone are not treated as obstacles on the way to it – you are walking to the desk chair space, not around it. Use zones marked as walk-through, like the strip beside a bed or the work aisle in front of a counter, never block a route: you can stand in them, you just should not put furniture there.

Why 36 inches is the reference line

The planner compares every walkway with the 36 in (915 mm) minimum clear width for walking surfaces in section 403.5.1 of the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design. Private homes are generally not required to meet those standards, and the planner does not claim they are. The number is used because it is a published, measured width that lets one adult walk past furniture without turning sideways, and the same 36 in figure appears as the recommended walkway width in the NKBA Kitchen Planning Guidelines (guideline 7). The standards also allow short pinch points: 32 in for no more than 24 in of length. If your narrowest point is a short squeeze past a nightstand, the planner will still show it below 36 in – you decide whether that is acceptable for you.

Working with the planner

  • Measure the inside faces of the walls, not the outside of the trim. Doors are measured as the clear opening with the door open 90°, as described in ADA 2010 §404.2.3.
  • Drag pieces with a mouse or finger, or select one with Tab and move it with the arrow keys (Shift for 6 in steps). R rotates, Delete removes.
  • Snapshots A and B under “Compare two versions” let you try a second arrangement and see free floor, narrowest walkway and conflicts side by side.
  • Export the drawing as SVG for printing or sending to a landlord, or as JSON to continue on another device. Import accepts the same JSON.
  • Switch the units at any time; the plan is stored in inches internally and converted exactly (1 in = 2.54 cm).

What the planner does not do

It treats every piece as a rectangle at floor level, so a wall-mounted shelf above a desk, a radiator under a window or a sloped ceiling is not modeled. It does not know about building codes, fire egress, the swing of a window sash or the weight of anything. It is a geometric aid that makes the space between objects visible – read more in About and method.

Frequently asked questions

Is the planner free and do I need an account?

Yes, it is free and there is no account. Your plan is stored only in your own browser (localStorage) and can be exported as JSON or SVG.

Why is my walkway shown as narrower than the gap I measured?

The planner adds use zones: a desk chair, an open drawer or the swing of the door take space even when they are not drawn as furniture. Select the piece to see or reduce its zone.

Can I use centimeters?

Yes. Use the in/cm switch. All inputs and results change unit; the stored plan keeps full precision.

Does a result of 36 in mean my home is accessible?

No. 36 in is a reference taken from ADA 2010 §403.5.1. Accessibility also depends on turning space, door hardware, thresholds and much more that the planner does not model.
