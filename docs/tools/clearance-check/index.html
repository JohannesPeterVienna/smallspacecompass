Clearance Check

Four quick sums for the questions that come up before any drawing: is there room beside the bed, is this gap still a walkway, how wide may the table be, is the kitchen aisle wide enough? Each result shows its working and is compared with published widths from five primary documents, each linked to its entry in the reference table.

Nothing is stored or uploaded. “Copy link” puts your numbers into the link itself, after the # sign; whoever opens it sees the same numbers.

What the check does

Each of the four checks is a subtraction you could do on paper – the value of the tool is that it shows the working, compares the result with every published width that applies, and tells you how much is missing for the next one. It works on one section through the room: a wall with a bed against it, a gap with use zones on either side, a room with a table in it, a kitchen with one or two counter runs.

The use zones in the walkway check

A gap is not a walkway until you subtract what happens in it. The check offers three zone depths. The 32 in for a desk or dining chair is the distance the NKBA guideline gives from a table edge to a wall where nobody passes. The 18 in for an open drawer or sofa legroom and the 24 in bedside strip are this site’s own planning assumptions – no standard we could read states them – so they are labelled as such wherever they appear. If your chair, drawer or door leaf is deeper, subtract it by hand and enter the smaller gap.

Reading the result

The list of reference widths is sorted from the smallest to the largest. A row says “meets” when your clear width is at least that wide, and gives the margin either way. The documents were written for different purposes: an accessibility standard for public buildings, a federal standard for factory-built homes, English building-regulations guidance, a trade association’s kitchen guidelines. None of them governs how you furnish a private room. Together they bracket the range from “a person can physically pass” to “comfortable for a wheelchair user”, which is more useful than a single rule of thumb. The reference table shows each figure with its section number, scope and link.

“Next step” names the largest piece – or the smallest room – that would still reach the next width up, so you know what to change. A sum on one section cannot see door swings, a second piece of furniture further along or a route that turns a corner; for that, draw the room in the Clearance Planner.

Limits

  • One straight section, rectangles only. Skirting boards, radiators, handles and bed frames wider than the mattress have to be included in the numbers you type.
  • Metric figures from the English documents are compared exactly (750 mm = 29.5 in); inch figures from the U.S. documents likewise. Results are rounded to 0.1 in or 0.1 cm for display only.
  • Meeting a width here is not a statement that a room complies with any code or accessibility standard.

Frequently asked questions

Is 24 inches beside a bed a rule?

No. It is this site’s planning assumption. The only bedside figure we found in a primary document is the 750 mm (29.5 in) clear access zone of England’s Approved Document M for accessible and adaptable dwellings, and 1000 mm for wheelchair user dwellings; the check shows both.

Why does the kitchen check list both 40 and 42 inches?

They come from different documents: 40 in is the minimum clearance in a pass-through kitchen in the 2010 ADA Standards (§804.2.1), 42 in is the work aisle the NKBA recommends for one cook (guideline 6).

What does “Copy link” contain?

The check you chose, the unit and the numbers you typed, written after the # sign of the link. Browsers do not send that part to the web server. Nothing is stored on this site.
