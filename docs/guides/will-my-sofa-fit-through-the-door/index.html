Will my sofa fit through the door? 5 measurements

Most sofas that get stuck do not fail at the front door; they fail at the first hallway corner. Five measurements tell you in advance whether a sofa will make it, and which way round to carry it.

Last reviewed: October 20265 min readUnits: in + cm

Why sofas get stuck

A sofa is a long box with a short cross-section. Through a straight doorway only the cross-section matters – depth and height – because the length is simply carried through. In a hallway that turns 90°, the length suddenly matters, because the sofa has to swing round the corner. That is why a sofa that passes the front door can still be stuck one meter later. The five measurements below cover both cases.

1. The door's clear width

32 inMinimum door clear width (815 mm)ADA 2010 §404.2.3 – measured face of door to stop, door open 90°

Do not measure the door leaf. Open the door to 90° and measure from the face of the open door to the stop on the opposite jamb. This is how §404.2.3 of the 2010 ADA Standards defines the clear width of a doorway, and it is usually 2 in or more less than the leaf width because the open door and its hinges take space. Many interior doors in older homes are narrower than 32 in. If you can lift the door off its hinges, you gain the thickness of the leaf and the hinge knuckles.

2. The door's clear height

From the floor – or the top of the threshold – to the underside of the head stop. Standard doors are often around 80 in high, but measure yours: older buildings, basements and doors with closers vary. Height matters when the sofa has to be stood on end.

3. The sofa's depth, height and diagonal depth

Take the overall depth (front of arm to back), overall height (floor to top of back) and the diagonal depth: from the side, the straight distance from the top back corner of the frame to the front edge of the seat. A sofa with a sloping back can be tipped onto its back or end and “rolled” through a doorway that is narrower than its depth, provided the diagonal depth is smaller than the door's clear width. Furniture retailers often list this figure; otherwise use a straightedge across the side profile.

4. Hallway widths at every corner

Measure wall to wall before and after each turn, at the narrowest point – radiators, skirting, light switches and door frames count. The longest flat rectangle of width w that can turn a right-angled corner between halls of width a and b follows from a classic geometry problem: it is the minimum of a/cos θ + b/sin θ − w(tan θ + cot θ) over all angles θ. The numbers drop quickly as the item gets wider: in two 36 in halls, a 24 in wide box can be up to about 54 in long; a 30 in wide one only about 42 in. That is why a sofa is usually carried on its side or on its end through corners – it reduces the width that has to turn.

5. Headroom at the corner

If the sofa will not turn flat, movers tip one end up. Raising the long axis shortens the sofa's footprint, but only as far as the ceiling allows. Measure floor to ceiling at the corner, and note light fittings, smoke alarms and soffits. On stairs, measure the headroom at the landing where the turn happens; it is often lower than in the hallway.

Worked example

A three-seat sofa measures 84 in long, 37 in deep and 34 in high, with a diagonal depth of 31 in. The apartment door has a 32 in clear width and 80 in clear height; the hallway behind it turns 90° with 36 in on both sides and a 96 in ceiling.

  • Door: the sofa's depth (37 in) and height (34 in) are both wider than the door, so it will not go through upright or on its back. Tipped on its back and rolled through, the 31 in diagonal depth clears the 32 in opening with 1 in to spare – tight but possible.
  • Corner: carried on its back or side, the cross-section is 34 × 37 in, which leaves almost nothing to swing in a 36 in hall. Stood on end, its 37 × 34 in footprint just misses the 36 × 36 in corner square by 1 in. Result: it fails at the corner by about an inch even though it passes the door.

The fix is to ask the retailer before ordering: legs and back cushions that come off, arms that unbolt, or a modular design. A 2 in difference in depth would have been enough here.

Before delivery day

  • Take the measurements yourself and send them to the retailer; ask for their minimum doorway figure for the exact model.
  • Remove doors from hinges along the route, and remove door stops if the gain matters.
  • Protect corners and the floor; a sofa that “just fits” is often carried at an awkward angle.
  • For stairs with winders or spiral stairs, no 2D check is reliable – ask for a site survey.

Frequently asked questions

How wide does a door need to be for a sofa?

Wider than either the sofa’s depth or height, or – for a sofa tipped on its back or end – wider than its diagonal depth. Measure the door’s clear width with the door open 90°, not the leaf.

What is the diagonal depth of a sofa?

The straight-line distance, seen from the side, from the top back corner of the frame to the front edge of the seat. It decides whether a sofa can be tipped through a narrow doorway.

Can you take a door off to fit a sofa?

Usually yes. Lifting the leaf off its hinges adds roughly the thickness of the door and the hinge knuckles to the clear width; removing the stops can add a little more.

Why did my sofa fit through the door but not the hallway?

Through a straight doorway only the cross-section matters. Around a corner the sofa’s length has to swing, and in narrow halls the maximum length that can turn falls quickly as the item gets wider.
