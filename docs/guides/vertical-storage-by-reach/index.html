Storage

Vertical storage by reach height

Floor space is what small homes lack; wall height is what they have. But not every inch of wall is equally useful. Plan storage in three bands – below 15 in, 15–48 in, above 48 in – and put things where you will actually reach them.

Last reviewed: November 20265 min readUnits: in + cm

The reach range

15–48 inUnobstructed reach range (380–1220 mm)ADA 2010 §308.2.1 (forward) and §308.3.1 (side)

Sections 308.2.1 and 308.3.1 of the 2010 ADA Standards set the unobstructed forward and side reach for operable parts and storage at 15 in minimum and 48 in maximum above the floor. That is a legal accessibility minimum for an unobstructed reach, written for people using wheelchairs. Over an obstruction the limits drop: 44 in for a forward reach deeper than 20 in (§308.2.2), 46 in for a side reach deeper than 10 in (§308.3.2). This guide uses 15–48 in as a planning band, not as a promise that everything inside it is easy to reach for everyone: a deep shelf, a counter in front of a cabinet or a person's own height and mobility change the real reach. Above 48 in, most standing adults still reach – less comfortably, and not over a deep counter or shelf. Below 15 in, you bend or kneel.

What goes in each band

BandHeightStore here
Highabove 48 inseasonal clothing, suitcases, spare bedding, archive boxes, things used monthly or less
Everyday15–48 indaily clothes, shoes in use, keys and bags, kitchen staples, books you read, chargers
Lowbelow 15 inheavy items (tools, drinks), shoes, baskets, things children use

The principle is frequency: the more often you use something, the closer it should be to the middle of the everyday band – roughly between hip and shoulder height. Heavy things go low regardless of frequency, because lifting them from above shoulder height is where accidents happen.

Two lessons from such audits: tall units put a large share of their volume – here almost two-fifths – above 48 in, and the everyday band holds only about a third, even on a wall that looks full. If most of your daily things do not fit in the everyday band, the answer is usually wider rather than taller storage – another 24 in of wall at 15–48 in adds more useful volume than a cabinet extension to the ceiling.

Shelf depth

Depth decides whether the high band is usable at all, and here the ADA Standards give exact figures. Over an obstruction, the highest side reach stays at 48 in only while the reach depth is 10 in or less; between 10 and 24 in of depth it drops to 46 in (§308.3.2). For a forward reach, the limit is 48 in up to 20 in of depth and 44 in beyond, with 25 in as the deepest reach allowed (§308.2.2). In practice: reaching sideways over a dresser 24 in deep and up to 34 in high, the limit of that standard is 46 in, not 48 – and over anything deeper or higher it gives no figure at all.

Two more published depths are useful when you size a unit. The U.S. manufactured-home standard requires clothes-hanging space at least 22 in deep inside (24 CFR 3280.109(c)), and Approved Document M draws a double wardrobe 600 mm (23.6 in) deep and a chest of drawers 450 mm (17.7 in) deep in its furniture schedule (Appendix D). For books, shoes and boxes we found no published depth: measure what you store and add a finger’s width.

Depth also costs floor. A 16 in deep cabinet on a walkway takes 4 in more than a 12 in bookcase; see the walkway guide.

Spacing shelves

Put a shelf at about 48 in where possible – the top of the everyday band – and divide the band below it to match what you store: measure the tallest thing that goes on each shelf and add about an inch to get it in and out. Space above 48 in in bigger steps for boxes. If you are building a unit with equal compartments, the divider calculator in the Storage Wall Audit gives clear widths and marking positions.

Doors, drawers and the floor in front

Cupboard doors and drawers need space in front of them: about one door leaf or one drawer depth plus standing room. A wall of cabinets along a 36 in walkway is fine with closed doors and blocks the route the moment one is open. Sliding doors and open shelves avoid that – at the cost of dust and visual clutter.

Heights other documents give

The 15–48 in band is not the only published height range. These come from the same documents and are collected in the reference table:

The English range for switches (450–1200 mm) is almost the same band as the ADA reach range (380–1220 mm): two regulators arrived at nearly the same everyday zone. The NKBA line at 84 in marks the other end – storage above it is not counted as wall, base or pantry storage at all. Whatever the height, anchor tall units to the wall, and keep heavy things low.

A quick audit of your own walls

Take one wall at a time. List what is on each shelf and how often you use it: daily, weekly, monthly, seasonally. Anything daily that sits above 48 in or below 15 in is a candidate to move. Anything seasonal at chest height is taking the best space. To put numbers on it, enter the wall in the Storage Wall Audit and export the per-unit volumes as CSV.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best height for everyday storage?

Between 15 and 48 in above the floor – the unobstructed reach range in ADA 2010 §308.2.1 and §308.3.1. Put the most-used items around hip to shoulder height.

How deep should shelves be?

Match what you store. Published figures exist for clothes: at least 22 in inside depth for hanging space in the U.S. manufactured-home standard (24 CFR 3280.109), and 600 mm for a wardrobe in England’s Approved Document M. Keep high shelves shallow: under the ADA Standards the high side reach drops from 48 to 46 in once the reach depth exceeds 10 in.

Is storage up to the ceiling worth it?

Yes for rarely used things, but it holds less useful volume than it seems. If daily items do not fit, add width at reach height first.

How do I calculate shelf volume?

Width × depth × height of each unit, divided by 1728 for cubic feet. The Storage Wall Audit does this per reach band.
