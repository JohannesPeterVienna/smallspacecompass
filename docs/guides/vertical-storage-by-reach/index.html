Storage

Vertical storage by reach height

Floor space is what small homes lack; wall height is what they have. But not every inch of wall is equally useful. Plan storage in three bands – below 15 in, 15–48 in, above 48 in – and put things where you will actually reach them.

Last reviewed: October 20265 min readUnits: in + cm

The reach range

15–48 inUnobstructed reach range (380–1220 mm)ADA 2010 §308.2.1 (forward) and §308.3.1 (side)

Sections 308.2.1 and 308.3.1 of the 2010 ADA Standards set the unobstructed forward and side reach for operable parts and storage at 15 in minimum and 48 in maximum above the floor. The range was written for people using wheelchairs, but it works as a comfort zone for everyone: inside it, you reach without bending deeply or stretching up. Above 48 in, most standing adults still reach – just less comfortably, and not over a deep counter or shelf. Below 15 in, you bend or kneel.

What goes in each band

BandHeightStore here
Highabove 48 inseasonal clothing, suitcases, spare bedding, archive boxes, things used monthly or less
Everyday15–48 indaily clothes, shoes in use, keys and bags, kitchen staples, books you read, chargers
Lowbelow 15 inheavy items (tools, drinks), shoes, baskets, things children use

The principle is frequency: the more often you use something, the closer it should be to the middle of the everyday band – roughly between hip and shoulder height. Heavy things go low regardless of frequency, because lifting them from above shoulder height is where accidents happen.

Two lessons from such audits: tall units put a large share of their volume – here almost two-fifths – above 48 in, and the everyday band holds only about a third, even on a wall that looks full. If most of your daily things do not fit in the everyday band, the answer is usually wider rather than taller storage – another 24 in of wall at 15–48 in adds more useful volume than a cabinet extension to the ceiling.

Shelf depth

Depth matters more than people expect. A 16 in deep shelf above 48 in is hard to use: you cannot see or reach what is at the back. Common depths:

  • Books, folded shirts: 10–12 in.
  • Shoes: 12–14 in.
  • Hanging clothes: about 24 in for a rail with hangers.
  • Boxes and bins: match the box; standard archive boxes are around 12–15 in deep.
  • Above 48 in: keep it shallow, or use pull-down rails or boxes with labelled fronts.

Depth also costs floor. A 16 in deep cabinet on a walkway takes 4 in more than a 12 in bookcase; see the walkway guide.

Spacing shelves

Put a shelf at about 48 in where possible – the top of the everyday band – and divide the band below it to match what you store: 11–13 in for books and shoes, 14–16 in for folded clothes. Space above 48 in in bigger steps for boxes. If you are building a unit with equal compartments, the divider calculator in the Storage Wall Audit gives clear widths and marking positions.

Doors, drawers and the floor in front

Cupboard doors and drawers need space in front of them: about one door leaf or one drawer depth plus standing room. A wall of cabinets along a 36 in walkway is fine with closed doors and blocks the route the moment one is open. Sliding doors and open shelves avoid that – at the cost of dust and visual clutter.

Safety

  • Anchor every tall unit to the wall; a loaded bookcase can tip when a drawer or door is pulled.
  • Keep heavy items low, especially above beds and desks.
  • Use a stable step stool for the high band rather than a chair.

Room by room

Kitchen: upper cabinets usually start around 18 in above the counter, so their lowest shelf sits at about 54 in – above the reach band. Keep everyday plates and glasses on the lowest upper shelf or in drawers below the counter, and use the top shelves for rarely used dishes. Bedroom: a wardrobe rail at about 64–68 in is above 48 in by design, because clothes hang down from it; folded clothes belong in the band below. Entry: keys, bags and the dog lead go between 15 and 48 in, coats higher – see entryway storage. Bathroom: daily toiletries at basin height, spare stock above or below.

A quick audit of your own walls

Take one wall at a time. List what is on each shelf and how often you use it: daily, weekly, monthly, seasonally. Anything daily that sits above 48 in or below 15 in is a candidate to move. Anything seasonal at chest height is taking the best space. Most people find that a single reshuffle frees a whole shelf in the everyday band – without buying anything.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best height for everyday storage?

Between 15 and 48 in above the floor – the unobstructed reach range in ADA 2010 §308.2.1 and §308.3.1. Put the most-used items around hip to shoulder height.

How deep should shelves be?

10–12 in for books and folded shirts, 12–14 in for shoes, about 24 in for hanging clothes. Keep high shelves shallow so you can see and reach the back.

Is storage up to the ceiling worth it?

Yes for rarely used things, but it holds less useful volume than it seems. If daily items do not fit, add width at reach height first.

How do I calculate shelf volume?

Width × depth × height of each unit, divided by 1728 for cubic feet. The Storage Wall Audit does this per reach band.
